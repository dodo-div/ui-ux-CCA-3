Conclusion:

Overview

This study compared the original FamPay interface with the redesigned FamApp. Five participants (A to E) used both versions and described what felt good and bad. Three of them tried the original first and two tried the redesign first, which reduced the chance that the order of use would bias their opinions. Each participant completed everyday tasks: checking the balance, paying at the canteen, adding money, reviewing spending, and understanding transaction statuses. Their feedback was consistent. All five preferred the redesign and described it as cleaner, calmer and easier to use.

Problems with the Original FamPay

Participants described the original app as busy, crowded and tiring. Offers, banners, cards and icons competed for attention, so users had to hunt for basic information such as their available balance. Some icons were not understood, and participants guessed and sometimes guessed wrong. Text was small and the layout felt cramped, which made users worry about pressing the wrong thing. Payment outcomes were not always clear, so people were unsure whether a transaction had gone through. These problems match several heuristic principles: simplicity, human limitations, linguistic clarity and responsiveness.

What the Redesign Did Well

Clear visual hierarchy. The large white balance on the dark card was the first thing every participant noticed. Participants A and C said they understood their balance immediately with no searching. The orange Scan & Pay button was seen as the obvious primary action, and A completed the canteen payment in two taps.

Efficient daily actions. The row of quick buttons (Pay, Scan, Your QR, Add Money, History) put frequent tasks in one place. Participants described this as smart and time-saving. B added money without any help, which was not possible in the original.

Useful spending insight. The “This Month” card, showing ₹3,240 spent of ₹5,000 with a colour-coded Food, Shopping, Entertainment and Other breakdown, was one of the most praised features. Participant A saw within seconds that most money goes to food, and D said it makes them think before spending. In the original, this would require opening the history and adding things up manually.

Motivation and personalisation. The Keeper card, with its Art Supplies goal and 33% progress bar, made saving feel achievable. The friendly greeting (“Good morning, Ananya” and “You’re on track this month”) made the app feel supportive. The “For You” offer matched to food spending felt personal and not like random advertising.

Readable and organised content. Clear section headings (This Month, Recent Activity, Coming Up, For You) helped participant E always know where they were. The logical order, from balance to actions to spending to history, matched how users naturally think. The transaction list showed a name, category, amount and status together, and the “Coming Up” section helped users remember subscription payments such as Spotify on Oct 12.

Problems Found in the Redesign

Despite the strong response, participants identified several issues that should be fixed.

Duplicate and unclear labels. “Pay” appears both in the orange card and in the quick-action row, and “Pay” and “Scan & Pay” sound almost the same. A and E both found this confusing. Names such as “Keeper”, the difference between “Wallet” and “Home”, and the small “CARD” tag on the Spotify payment were also unclear without extra explanation.

Small and low-contrast text. The labels under the quick-action icons, the bottom navigation labels, the grey secondary text (such as “Subscription” and “UPI”) and the orange “View all” link were all described as too small or too light. The icons were also close enough together that A mis-tapped Scan. This is especially important because the app is meant for frequent, quick use.

Weak and non-actionable statuses. The Failed and Pending labels were in light orange or grey and did not stand out. More importantly, they gave no reason and no next step. A, C and D all wanted to know why a payment failed and to retry it. A failed payment is a critical event, so it deserves a stronger colour, an explanation and a clear action.

Missing safeguards and states. C wanted a confirmation step before a payment is sent, to avoid mistakes. D noted there was no search to find older payments. E pointed out that error and empty states, such as zero balance or no internet, were not designed. The active tab in the bottom navigation was also only slightly highlighted, making it hard to tell the current screen.

Long page. E felt that the long scroll made it easy to forget what was at the top, and suggested collapsing some sections.

Recommendations

Based on the feedback, the following improvements are recommended:

Merge or rename the duplicate Pay and Scan & Pay buttons so each action has one clear name.
Increase the size and contrast of small text, links and navigation labels, and add more spacing between quick-action icons.
Show Failed and Pending in stronger colours (for example red for failed), with a reason and a Retry or Details option.
Rename or explain unclear terms such as Keeper and CARD, and clarify the difference between Wallet and Home, possibly with a short tooltip.
Add a payment confirmation step, a search option, and designs for error and empty states.
Make the active navigation tab more visible and consider collapsing less important sections to shorten the page.
Final Remarks

Overall, the redesign succeeds in its main goal. It reduces clutter, highlights the most important information and actions, and gives students insight into their spending and saving habits. Participants found it faster, friendlier and easier to understand than the original FamPay, and several said it already feels like a real product. The remaining issues are mostly refinements to labels, text size, status feedback and safeguards, not fundamental design problems. Addressing them would make FamApp more accessible, more trustworthy and more complete, and would bring it closer to the heuristic principles of user control, consistency, forgiveness and responsiveness.
